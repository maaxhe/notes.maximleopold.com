# Five Myths About the Brain

When I get asked about the brain, these five myths tend to come up most often.  
These are the most common myths about the brain I came across. Here are short explanations of why they are wrong. Enjoy!

---

## 1. The Brain Mainly Processes Inputs

Not really. The brain is primarily in the business of **predicting its input**, not simply processing it.  
Neurons compete in the brain for the best possible connections to other neurons.

Neurons are built to always minimise energy consumption. This works best when a neuron can **predict its input**. Therefore, neurons compete in responding faster to another neuron’s firing. This creates a highly efficient network in the brain.  
These local predictions form global predictions about the outside world. Many predictions together form a **model of reality**.

- If the internal model matches reality → little neural activity (energy efficiency).
- If there is surprise → neurons fire strongly, making learning and memory more likely.

**Example:**  
You have an internal model of driving. You predict that the road makes a right turn. Suddenly, behind the turn, there is a traffic jam and you have to brake hard.  
That surprise ensures you will remember to be more careful next time.

---

## 2. Dopamine is the Pleasure Hormone

It’s not for pleasure, and it’s not a hormone!  
Dopamine is a **neurotransmitter** involved in many things, mainly **wanting more** (craving).

- Dopamine is at its highest **before** a reward (anticipation).
- It spikes at **surprise** (see Myth 1) and helps the brain remember this event.
- Next time, dopamine release is smaller, because the brain can now predict it.

Dopamine also plays crucial roles in **motor control** and **decision-making**.

---

## 3. Memories are Engraved in the Brain

No, memory doesn’t stay the same.  
Memories are **dynamic and malleable** — they change every time you recall them.  
Current feelings and perspectives mix with old memories and reshape them.

The ultimate purpose of memory is not to preserve the past but to **better predict the future**:  
_Where will I find food? Is this a foe or a friend?_

Within the predictive framework (see Myth 1), memory is about **optimising predictions**, not archiving the past.

---

## 4. We are Masters of Our Destiny

“I don’t get why people end up on the streets. They are responsible for their own fate” — I hear this often.

This is why Robert Sapolsky dedicated two books, _Behave_ and _Determined_, to this topic.  
We think we have free will, but reality is far more complicated:

- Fetal development, upbringing, and early social circles strongly shape outcomes.
- These factors influence vulnerability to drugs, mental health, and life choices.

There is **less freedom** in our personality than we imagine.  
But there is still **room for agency and change**. The best start: **change the environment**, which then shapes behaviour.

---

## 5. Setting an Alarm to Study is Better than Sleeping

“I set an alarm to start studying early” — a common mistake.  
This relies on the false belief that more study time always equals better results.

- **Sleep restriction** during exam periods impairs next-day performance.
- It also reduces **memory consolidation** from the previous day.
- You may feel efficient, but actually learn slower — like a phone showing “100%” battery, but really only having 91% capacity after many cycles.

The culprit: residual **adenosine** blocking receptors in the brain.

---

## Final Words

Mainly, if you think about the brain as a **prediction machine**, everything else fits this framework.  
Think of the brain as a **highly efficient and flexible computer** — capable of generalisation, creativity, and adaptation.

Keep it safe and healthy!  
Remember: the **best medicine** for the brain (and body) is **sleep** — it improves learning, wellbeing, and future predictions.

See you next time 🙂
