# Is my Smartphone Part of my Mind?

## A story about how smartphones change our brains

The only time I’m more than three meters away from my phone is when I’m sailing.  
Once a week, out on the water, I’m offline. No messages, no maps, no news. And honestly? It’s a relief.

But let’s be real: the rest of the week, I need my phone constantly. It holds my bus ticket, my calendar, my gym bookings. It’s my memory, my map, my communication.

So… is it still a tool or has it become part of me?

---

## The Phone as a Cognitive Extension

Clark & Chalmers wrote about this in 1998 (the year I was born — and still 9 years before the first iPhone).  
Their idea? If you imagine rotating a cup in your head or a picture of a cup with your fingers on a screen, same outcome, same process.  
So why not treat the screen as part of the thinking?

Picture me (or a friend), trying to find a train station. Either I memorise the route, or I follow Google Maps like a brainless zombie. Same result. I navigate. My brain says: task complete.

According to the Extended Mind thesis, your phone isn’t just helping your mind, it’s part of it.  
This is called the **Parity Principle**.

---

## But wait! Can a device really be a part of _You_?

Let’s find a good analogy:

- Is a USB stick part of the computer?  
  If the software of your program is stored on the stick, then yes — it functionally extends the computer’s memory.

- Same goes for your smartphone. When you’re using it to think, to remember, to decide, it might act like an additional limb.

BUT, you could say, a USB stick is removable and a smartphone is just resting in your hands – therefore not really part of the main system.

True! But then again, are the side mirrors of a car part of the car itself? Most people would say yes.  
What about a trailer? Only when attached, of course!

So maybe the rule is not “connected or not” but rather **“functionally integrated or not.”**

---

## But Your Brain already Works Like This

Suppose you break your ankle (hopefully not!).  
In the first days you might still try to walk normally out of habit — ouch, that hurts!

Why? Because your **Basal Ganglia**, the brain structure that controls habitual movements, has not adapted yet.  
Over time though, the Basal Ganglia updates its inner model and won’t let you step on that foot.

Same with your phone: carry it around long enough, and your brain predicts it’s always there — like a body part.  
It’s almost a reflex for me to reach for my phone once I sit down on the bus.  
I even get anxious when it’s missing, because my brain’s prediction (phone in pocket) is violated.

---

## Phantom (Limb) Vibration Syndrome

You’ve probably had this happen: you feel your phone vibrate, you check, but nothing’s there.  
That was likely a **phantom vibration**.

Your brain is so used to expecting it, that it misinterprets another sensory input (like a muscle twitch) as a notification.  
It predicted the vibration so strongly that it _overwrote reality_.

A glitch in your predictive model — and a great example of how tightly our minds and phones have become interconnected.

I sometimes imagine the brain as having a “negative mold” of my phone inside it, waiting for input.  
And when the input doesn’t come, something feels missing.

---

## Final Thought

So maybe losing your phone doesn’t feel awful just because you miss pulling it out on the bus.  
Maybe it’s because your brain has come to treat the phone as part of _you_.

The **Extended Mind** idea is not science fiction.  
It shows how technology doesn’t just help us — it rewires us.

It is the world we live in.
