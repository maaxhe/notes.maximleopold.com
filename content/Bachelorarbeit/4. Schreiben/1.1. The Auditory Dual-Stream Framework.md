# 1.1. The Auditory Dual-Stream Framework
## 1.1.1 The Visual Two-Streams Hypothesis
The concept of parallel processing streams in the brain was first established in the visual system. Based on lesion studies in macaque monkeys, Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982) [Quelle] described two anatomically distinct pathways originating in the primary visual cortex (V1): a ventral stream projecting toward inferotemporal cortex, and a dorsal stream projecting toward posterior parietal cortex. Damage to the ventral pathway impaired the ability to identify *what* an object was - missing the ability to define object identities like differentiating between a rectangle and a triangle) - while damage to the dorsal pathway impaired the ability to track *where* objects are located in space. Goodale & Milner [Quelle checken] (1992) later refined this framework, proposing that the dorsal stream primarily supports visuomotor control - guiding *how* to interact with objects - while the ventral stream serves object perception and recognition. Despite ongoing debate about precise function labels, the distinction between ventral what-pathway and dorsal where/how-stream remains foundational to visual cortical organization. 

Critical for the present thesis is recent work, that identified prefrontal nodes that perform top-down control over each stream. Drawing on evidence from functional and structural connectivity, Bedini and Baldauf (2021) demonstrated a clear dissociation in intrinsic functional connectivity: The **Frontal Eye Field (FEF)** is a core node of the Dorsal Attention Network (DAN), showing predominant coupling with regions of the dorsal visual where-stream, while the **anterior Inferior Frontal Junction (IFJa)** is part of the Frontoparietal Network (FPN) and couples preferentially with the ventral visual what-stream. This connectivity-based division of labor was subsequently supported by resting-state MEG data showing the same dissociation in oscillatory coupling ([[Soyuhos, O., & Baldauf, D. (2022)]]). Together, these findings suggest a functional specialization in prefrontal top-down control - one that the present thesis asks whether it extends to the auditory modality. 
## 1.1.2 From Wernicke's Area to Auditory Dual-Stream Processing

For over a century, the neural basis of auditory language processing was thought to operate in a single cortical region - Wernicke's area. It is located in the posterior left superior temporal gyrus (STG) and was considered the primary hub for auditory comprehension following Wernicke's (1874) findings in receptive aphasia. This model dominated neuroscience well into the twentieth century. 

The model began to be challenged in the 1970s and 1980s, when neuropsychological evidence revealed that lesions to the left STG did not consistently produce comprehension deficits, but were instead more reliably associated with speech production deficits ([[Hickok & Poeppel 2007 - Nature]]). These findings suggested that auditory processing could not be reduced to a single region and led to a fundamental re-evaluation of the cortical auditory organization. 

The current consensus, developed most influentially by Hickok & Poeppel (2004, 2007) and Rauschecker & Scott (2009), puts the organization of the auditory cortex into two parallel processing streams analogous to those in the visual system. A **posterodorsal stream** extends from the superior temporal plane through parietal and premotor cortex and is associated with spatial processing and sensorimotor integration. An **anteroventral stream** projects from the STG forward through the temporal lobe toward inferior frontal regions and supports auditory object identification and semantic processing.
